The method

How to run AI agents you can trust.

Treat agents like staff, not scripts. Staff have a written job description, a level of trust they've earned, a manager who signs off on the risky things, and a paper trail. All four have direct equivalents in software.

The hard part isn't the task

Getting a language model to do a piece of operational work stopped being the hard part some time ago. Ask it to triage a ticket, reconcile two systems, draft the customer reply, and it will do a decent job. The hard part is everything around that: knowing what it's allowed to touch, catching the confident mistake before it reaches a customer, and being able to answer "who approved this?" three weeks later when someone asks.

A capable agent with no rules isn't an employee. It's an unsupervised contractor with your credentials and no memory of what it did.

Everything below is described at the level of the pattern. The specifics belong to the clients I built them for.

01

Every agent has a charter

A charter is one written page, kept next to the code, that says what one agent is for: the systems it may read, the systems it may change, the things it must never do, and who owns it. It is written in plain language because its real audience is the person who has to approve it, not the model.

The may not lines are the ones that earn their keep. They're usually specific and slightly odd, the kind of rule that exists because of one bad afternoon. "Never link an internal document to an outside recipient; attach it instead." Those lines are the institutional memory that would otherwise live in one person's head.

02

Autonomy is a ladder, and it's earned

A single "is this agent allowed to act" switch is too blunt. Four levels cover nearly everything. An agent starts at the bottom and moves up on its record, not on how impressive its demo was.

Observe
Reads systems, reports findings. Cannot change anything anywhere. Every new agent starts here.
Draft
Prepares the work (the reply, the order, the reconciliation) but a person sends it. Most of the value shows up at this level, and a lot of agents never need to leave it.
Act with approval
Does routine work inside a defined lane on its own, and stops at a gate for anything that leaves the building or crosses a limit.
Autonomous
Deliberately empty. The level exists so the ladder is honest about where it ends, and so that promoting something into it is a visible decision somebody has to make on purpose.

The ladder from Agent Floor. The empty top level is the point.

Demotion matters as much as promotion. A charter violation drops an agent a level, the same way you'd pull back responsibility from someone who made a serious mistake. It goes back up by working correctly at the lower level for a while.

03

Human gates on anything irreversible

A gate is a hard stop in front of a specific class of action: anything that reaches a customer or vendor, anything that moves money, anything that can't be undone. The agent does all the work up to that line, then presents the finished work and waits.

A gate that shows the actual email, names the rule that triggered the stop, and states what the agent wasn't sure about gets read. If your reviewers are clicking approve without reading, you don't have a gate. You have a speed bump and a false sense of safety.

04

A log nobody can edit, because someone will ask

Every action, every refusal and every human decision lands in a log that is written once and never edited. Not for compliance theater. For the ordinary Tuesday when a customer asks why they got a particular email, and the honest answer needs to include which agent drafted it, which rule let it through, and which person pressed send.

Example log

Refusals go in the log too, and they're the most useful entries in it. An agent that declined work is telling you either that a charter is too tight or that a real hazard exists. Both are worth knowing, and neither shows up if you only log successes.

  • Who's on shift and at what level, so a promotion is something you see, not something you find out about later.
  • What's waiting on me, oldest first. If this list is never empty, autonomy is set too low. If it's always empty, it's set too high.
  • What stopped, and why, quoted against the rule that fired. This is the panel that tells you whether the rules are working or just decorative.
  • The running log, one stream across all agents, because most real problems show up in the order of events rather than inside any single one.

The measure of the whole arrangement is simple: a supervisor should be able to walk away for an afternoon and, on returning, reconstruct exactly what happened and why, without asking anyone.

Where I'd start

Pick one job with real volume and a small cost when it goes wrong. Start a single agent on it at Observe. Move it to Draft once what it reports matches what your people see, and leave it there longer than feels necessary. Write the charter before writing any code. The first two weeks should produce more edits to the charter than to the code. If they don't, the charter isn't specific enough to be doing any work.
