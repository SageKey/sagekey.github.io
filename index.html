How it stays safe

Getting an agent to do the work is the easy part.

Most AI pilots don't fail on the task. They stall on four questions nobody can answer — and until they're answered, no sensible operations leader lets the thing near a live system. Every agent I build answers all four before it starts.

"What is it allowed to touch?"

A written charter

Plain language, in version control: what the agent may read, may write, and may never do. Your team approves it before any code runs.

"How much do we trust it?"

Earned autonomy

Every agent starts at the bottom and moves up on its track record. Most never need to leave Draft.

Observe Draft Act with approval
"What if it's wrong?"

Human gates

Anything that reaches a customer, moves money or can't be undone stops and waits — with the finished work and the rule that fired on screen.

"Who approved this?"

A log nobody can edit

Every action, refusal and human decision is written once. Three weeks later, the answer is a lookup, not an investigation.

A capable agent with no governance isn't an employee. It's an unsupervised contractor with your credentials and no memory of what it did.

Services

Three ways in. Each one leaves you with something you keep.

Most engagements start small on purpose. The first question is whether an agent belongs in your operation at all, and that deserves a straight answer before anyone writes code.

01 · Start here

Agent Readiness Review

Short and fixed-scope. I look at your real queues, systems and volumes, and tell you which work is a genuine candidate, which isn't and why, and what running it would involve — including the parts that cost money and the parts that will annoy people.

You keep: a written plan and the first agent's charter, useful with or without me. If the answer is "not yet," it says so.
02 · The build

First agent in production

One agent on one real queue, with the controls built in rather than bolted on: charter, gates, log, and the oversight screen your team watches it through. It starts at Draft and moves up only on a track record.

You keep: a running agent, the governance documents in your own version control, and a team that knows how to supervise it.
03 · Ongoing

Oversight & expansion

Past two or three agents, the bottleneck becomes the person supervising them. This is the retained work: adding agents, tightening charters that proved too loose, loosening the ones strangling good work.

You keep: an operation that can add its next agent without me.

Fair questions

What you're probably thinking.

We tried AI and it produced something embarrassing.

Then the pilot was missing a gate, not a better model. My approach assumes the model will be confidently wrong on a regular basis — that's the design premise, not an edge case. The agent drafts, a person sends, and the log records which person. An agent at Draft can't embarrass you in front of a customer, because it never reaches one unaccompanied.

Our data is a mess, and half of it lives in the ERP.

That's the normal starting condition, and it's most of my background: years of moving order flow, shipping and inventory between systems that were never designed to talk to each other. If the data turns out to be the real problem, the Readiness Review will say so, and that's worth knowing before you spend on anything else.

Is this about replacing our people?

Not the way I scope it. The work that suits an agent is the queue nobody wants: retyping, copying between two screens, the reconciliation that eats a morning. The Draft rung keeps a person in the loop on purpose, and makes them the reviewer instead of the typist.

You're one person. What if you disappear?

It's the reason everything is built to be handed over. The charters are plain-language documents in your version control. The rules live in a policy engine outside the model, not in prompts only I understand. The oversight screen is for your team, not for me. If I vanish, you have a running system and the documents that explain it.

What does it cost?

The Readiness Review is a fixed price, agreed before it starts. A build is scoped from the review, so you see the number alongside the plan it pays for. I don't bill open-ended hours against a vague goal.
